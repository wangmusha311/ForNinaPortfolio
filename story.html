When I spill onto the streets again, I no longer take refuge in languid, dusk-time shadows. An hour, or perhaps two have passed since the shiny, plastic packaging of a kitkat bar was thrown away by the beautiful girl, forgotten on the pavement next to an old, rusty gate leading to a drab tenement yard, one of many that flaunt their nakedness of faded paint and chipped elevation here, downtown. I don’t bother with finding a right hiding spot now, because the entire city has turned into a smudge of summertime twilight that welcomes any and every shady character into its intestinally shaped streets. I walk close to the buildings along the way, preferring to feel their cool bodies pressed against the flesh of my arm, rather than the stifling air and the stifling blood ponding in the veins of masses that stroll through the very center of the road. It’s hot tonight. Even hotter than it was earlier, although it goes against logic for the night to be warmer than the day. It also goes against logic for our streets to be boiling under such heat, without break, both before and after the Sun goes down. Our people are not used to this kind of climate, so they must clench their yellowish teeth in non-air-conditioned living rooms and stew in the broth of their own, acidic sweat praying for the late Autumn to come and relieve them. But that won’t happen for at least three, long months. Let them swelter, I say. As we get closer and closer to Vashka’s, the crowd in the middle of the street swells. Pale legs exposed under the denim edges of the mini skirts, arms flexing, opaque with dust and dirt, poking out from the sleeveless tops. They stomp down, high heels clacking against the concrete, getting stuck in the thin rows between the cement tiles, sport shoes light and soundless, not leaving behind even a memory of a step. They flow through the wide street, and new members of this solemn army join the march every now and then, popping out from the gates that line the route to Vashka’s, growing and growing with each centimeter in density. Like a monster. Next to one of these gates, on a narrow windowsill of a kebab shop, I see an older woman sitting and clutching a bouquet of common flowers.






I stop and peak below the heavy, dyed fabric that covers her head and her nape. The cloth has a floral pattern similar, nearly identical, to the small cluster of herbs she holds close to her chest, the type you could find on the side of any provincial road. Sullied yellow heads like the two front teeth that remain stuck to the lady’s upper gum, hovering over the contour of her thin, dry lip, sagging petals, blue and violet like the city’s skyline ladened with gasoline fumes, strands of tired, green weeds like thin straps covering the shoulders of girls who trot in unstoppable masses towards Vashka’s gates. Colors of the flowers blend in with the blankness of the kebab shop’s front, with the grayness of the pavement, the darkening of the sky, but I quickly notice that the ancient peddler’s face isn’t as unappealing as would suggest her pathetic ware (and her lacking dental assortment.) Sure thing, she is old. Very old. Eyes barely floating on the creased surface of her wrinkled skin, drowning in the folds of old tissue, comically large nose an odd iceberg sailing on this ocean of human decay, wisps of hair, like rat tails, limp on her forehead. But I try to retrace and rearrange her features around a different time and a different space, erase the wrinkles, add shadow where the shadow’s needed, add highlight where the highlight’s needed to return her to her lost youth. And I wager, after some time of deliberation, that she must have been a beautiful woman once, and that arranged around a different time and space she didn’t paint such a depressing picture after all, and even her bouquet of nature’s mockery appeared to be worth a glance, retaining a spark of her former glory.
